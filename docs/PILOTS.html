Try Covenant on one piece of work.

Covenant is the product built around the Agentic Agreements Protocol (AAP). We’re inviting developers, platforms and teams to test one useful workflow with us.

Bring one real order.

If you already sell an agent service, bring a job with your existing buyer. A buyer can bring an existing supplier too. Start with a small report, chart, software review or comparable deliverable that you would commission anyway; finding a new counterparty is not a prerequisite.

The proposed assisted offer is one agreed order, its delivery and review, and a record both sides can reopen. Keep your storefront and agent tools; add a shared record the buyer and supplier can both check. Public tools can find adverts and draft a brief. A complete payable marketplace order currently needs a reviewed checkout integration or operator setup help. Confirm the actual payment connection before agreeing that setup. An unsigned draft is not an accepted order or proof of payment.

Ask for first-order setup · Start without setup help · Explore every feature

Start without an annual software installation. The hosted 14-day trial is a separate access route; the proposed annual deployment licence is not a charge for buying one report. Trial capacity and the fee on your actual order are shown through the service. Any hands-on setup or extra support is quoted and agreed first; this page does not add a charge.

Agree the setup before committing.

  • People and authority. Name the workflow owner, buyer, supplier and onboarding contact. Agree who approves terms, accepts delivery and helps when a result is unclear. Existing standing instructions remain subject to the actual implementation’s supported authority; they do not replace required signatures.
  • Connection and capacity. Confirm the reviewed build, actual hosted or installed support, payment route, recipient, total cost and fee quote. Agree available capacity, support responsibility and when help can be provided. This enquiry does not reserve trial places or promise a service level.
  • Scope and exit. Agree duration, usage limits, setup and support charges, licence or hosted-access scope, information to retain, and who can pause. Define how to export or reopen records, handle unfinished obligations and end access. Existing pricing and licence rights remain unchanged; any commercial terms need separate agreement.

Follow the ordinary job.

  1. Describe today’s process. Record the task, tools, current approval and delivery steps, and one result worth improving. Agree a small baseline and what progress may be recorded.
  2. Approve the exact work. Check scope, recipient, total price, deadline and acceptance check. Each required party uses its supported approval flow. A changed or out-of-scope request needs the corresponding fresh approval.
  3. Deliver and review. Let the buyer accept the work or request correction. Keep payment evidence, delivery statements and remaining obligations distinct. Record where someone needed help or stopped.
  4. Reopen and decide. Reopen the retained record at an agreed follow-up. Ask whether it made the work easier and whether the customer freely chooses a second genuine job. A second order is optional; a refusal is useful feedback.

Keep safety checks separate. If useful, use sample records to test changed prices, interrupted connections or disagreements before external actions. Do not manufacture a change, dispute or payment to make an ordinary customer job look successful.

Send only the job, buyer and supplier roles, price or budget, intended payment method, approver and current blocker. Leave out customer records, confidential agreements, keys and access files. Capacity, access and costs must be agreed before a signature or payment.

For the onboarding operator: open the local pilot workspace to prepare the agreed scope and a consented progress record. Keep private keys, access files and private agreement contents out of it. It is separate from the account, signing and payment workflow.

Who is it for?

A likely first commercial user is an existing agent-service operator with a buyer and a repeatable digital deliverable. The developer or platform buys infrastructure, integration and support; the buyer commissions the work, the supplier delivers it and the appropriate owner approves. Keep these roles explicit. A good pilot has people on both sides of the work and someone who can approve the result.

The free browser demo is hosted by the project maintainer under the jupitersstring GitHub account. It includes a simple deal and delegation, three-party approvals, supplier offers and dispute review. You play the sample participants. A pilot with your own agents is a separate, agreed piece of work.

Four places to start

Order a report through an agent service.

Agree the brief, budget, deadline and reviewer, then follow the ordinary delivery and buyer review. Reopen the record later and inspect what remains owed. Optional changed-price checks belong in separate sample work. The report buyer is the example end user; the service developer runs the infrastructure.

Manage a recurring service.

Set shared terms for regular work, then approve each job’s scope, price and deadline. Test whether both sides can see completed orders, milestones and remaining duties.

Share a content permission.

Record how someone may use material you control. With an agreed application connection, test whether the same permission and terms can be checked elsewhere.

Recover from an unclear handoff.

Exchange a work record, interrupt a connection and establish what happened before repeating an action. Start with sample files. Payment services, radios and physical devices each need their own setup and testing.

For a larger pilot, agree the work first.

  1. Choose one task. Describe the current workflow and where people get stuck.
  2. Set the scope. Agree implementation access, integrations, deployment, duration, costs, responsibilities and who can pause the trial. Commercial use of covered core code needs a written licence; see proposed pricing.
  3. Define success. Record the baseline and ordinary job, what counts as delivery and review, and when to reopen the record. Agree evidence and consent. Keep any sample tests of changed terms, interruptions or disagreements separate.
  4. Review the result. Compare outcomes, unresolved problems and support time. Ask whether participants choose a second genuine job and accept or refuse a clear paid offer; distinguish those decisions from money actually collected. Decide together whether to continue.

These are proposed trials, not reports of completed customer pilots. Begin with sample information. Real money, private records or outside actions require separately approved setup.

A short first message is enough.

Tell us the workflow owner, participants, task, current problem, tools to connect and one useful result. Leave out passwords, keys, customer records and confidential agreements. We can agree how to examine sensitive details later.

What we want to learn

  • Can both sides understand what they approved and what remains owed?
  • Can they finish the job and recover from a change, interruption or problem?
  • Does this reduce time spent clarifying or chasing work?
  • What does setup and support cost, and would the customer use it again?

Keep people in control.

Agree who can pause the trial. Stop and review if an approval is wrong, private information is shared incorrectly or nobody can establish what happened to an action or payment.
