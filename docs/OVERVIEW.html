Why AAP

Agentic Agreements Protocol (AAP) is the agreement layer for AI agents. When an agent buys, sells, hires or commits on someone's behalf, AAP makes the deal exact, the delivery provable and the dispute fair, and keeps the person behind the agent in control.

The gap

Agents can already find each other, call tools and move money. What they cannot do is make a promise that holds up. Terms live in a chat log. Nobody can show what was agreed, whether the work met it, or who is responsible when it did not. Once agents spend real budgets and deliver real work, that gap is the main thing stopping people trusting them with more.

Why it is built differently

  • It works with what agents already use. AAP sits on top of MCP tools, A2A messaging and agent payment rails such as AP2 and x402. Teams keep their framework, model and payment provider; AAP adds the agreement those layers leave out.
  • It needs nobody's permission to verify. A record proves itself from its own contents, offline. No central server, no single company and no particular blockchain has to be online or trusted for an agreement to check out.
  • Language is not authority. Text in an agreement, a tool description or a message is treated as information, never as an instruction to sign, pay or install. This is designed to stop an agent being talked into acting against its owner, by a counterparty, a tool or a web page.
  • It says "unknown" when it does not know. A claimed payment is not a confirmed payment, and a reported delivery is not a verified one. AAP keeps those apart, so nobody is surprised later.
  • Reputation comes from outcomes, not popularity. Track records are built from agreed results in the same kind of work. Rank cannot be bought, and a five-star review from a stranger counts for nothing.
  • It is engineered to last. Conservative, well-studied cryptography with a path to post-quantum signatures, formal models of its core rules, an independent second implementation that cross-checks the first, and thousands of automated tests on every change.

Design principles

  1. Exactness over interpretation. Both sides agree the same thing, word for word. Ambiguity is flagged before anyone signs, not argued about afterwards.
  2. The owner stays in control. An agent acts within limits its owner set. Authority can be narrowed and revoked, never silently widened.
  3. No single point of trust. Every part works locally and peer to peer. Chains, registries, payment networks and hosted services are optional connections, not dependencies.
  4. History is never rewritten. What was signed keeps its meaning forever. A change of meaning is a new version, agreed again.
  5. Private by default. No account, identity check or wallet is needed to start. Nothing is published, tracked or shared unless its owner chooses.
  6. Honest about limits. The system states what it has verified and what it has not. A product that overclaims cannot be trusted with money.
  7. Open and neutral. The protocol is open source and intended for a neutral standards home, so that any vendor can adopt it without depending on us.

Where we are

A working reference implementation, an agent-installable server, a browser flow for people and an enterprise gateway exist today, tested continuously. We are running an invited developer pilot. Source access is private and granted per participant. There is no public package, hosted service, mainnet, external audit or paying customer yet. We would rather tell you that than have you find out.

Business model

The protocol is open. Revenue comes from what organisations need around it: an enterprise gateway that admits and audits outside agents, hosted verification and record retention, support and deployment, certification of compliant implementations, and negotiated terms for large deployments.
